I’m increasingly working with coding agents across several machines, both local and remote, depending on the scope and intensity of the work.

If something is highly involved, I oversee it in real time, but roughly 90% of the work is fairly routine and can simply be pushed to a VM and left to run.

Across these environments, I tend to work with three kinds of agents. One is primarily for planning and reviews. Another is more of a higher-level reasoning and verification layer: useful for conceptualization, planning, reviews, and independently checking the outcomes produced by other agents. Then there is the main workhorse: the agent I use most heavily in VMs, where I can give it a task and largely let it run through to completion.

At the moment, I still orchestrate the interchange between these agents manually. When one agent finishes something important, I may ask another to review the result.

That review serves two purposes: it gives me an independent check on the outcome, but it is also my main observability point. By reading the review, I understand what was done, what matters, and whether the result is sound, without needing to follow the implementation itself.

I’m increasingly less concerned with how the work was carried out, as long as the specification was satisfied and the outcome survives independent review.

## Give the agent the whole computer

This is where I think the idea of the AI sandbox starts becoming more interesting.

When people first started building infrastructure for agents, a lot of the thinking was focused on systems. Even the language around runtimes suggested that these environments were primarily places for executing code generated by an agent.

But the obvious thing was also simply to run the agent inside the environment.

If you view environments as cheap, there are many benefits to giving an agent total control.

You can control the system in two ways: through observing inputs or through outputs. If we agree that compute and tokens are cheap—or at least that we can afford some inefficiency because everything is still so early—then it can be easier to control the system from the output side than to try to catch or create guardrails for every possible input.

That means we can say that computers are ephemeral and that we don’t care about anything except the outputs. And we can even afford to just have throwaway outputs.

Obviously, that does not apply equally to every kind of work. There are many shades of gray here. But for a large class of agentic work, especially work happening in isolated environments, this changes the framing considerably.

The concept of an agent in a computer is also easier to grasp.

For the majority of people, it’s very intuitive and easy to understand. This way, they can own it, make choices, and have this added freedom. Otherwise, you’re building a system for which you’re responsible end to end.

I think there’s a range of agentic applications, from very simple monitoring agents or various shapes of durable agents, asynchronous agents to very proactive, always-running agents.

For always-running agents, when you can afford to observe only the outputs and tolerate some inefficiency, agents inside a computer, or many agents spread across several computers, are an interesting framing.

## The body is not a box

But I don’t know if the computer itself is the product.

Agents need compute, but compute is just one of the resources. You have tokens, compute, and network. Let’s call the environment a sandbox for now. Or, to use the analogy that tools are hands for agents, we are building a body for an agent.

It’s something organic that can change and reshape for the need at hand.

That is the part I think is easy to miss when we talk about sandboxes.

<blockquote class="featured-quote unattributed">
    A sandbox sounds fixed. A computer sounds fixed. But the needs of an agent are not fixed.
</blockquote>

One task may need a lot of compute and very little network access. Another may need a browser. Another may need several environments at the same time. One agent may do the planning, another the implementation, and another may independently review the result.

The value, then, is in knowing, orchestrating, or having the control plane above this, where you can mix and match various types of agent needs versus the capabilities you’re giving it.

I think there’s a lot of space here for discovery and innovation.

Just having a computer is basically the same analogy as when you get a computer for yourself: it enables you, but it’s not prescriptive about what you will do with it.

And maybe that is exactly why the agent-in-a-computer framing is so useful. It gives the agent a general-purpose environment without forcing us to know in advance every possible thing the agent will need to do.

## One level up

But that also means the computer itself will be difficult to differentiate forever.

I don’t think the interesting long-term competition is simply who can provide the sandbox, runtime, or computer. Those things are likely to become increasingly interchangeable.

The more interesting layer is one level above.

It is the layer that understands what kind of work is happening, what resources an agent needs, how those resources should be assembled, which agent should do what, when another agent should review the result, and what actually matters in the output.

Today, I still do much of that orchestration myself.

I decide when something deserves close supervision and when it can just be pushed to a machine and left to run. I decide when an important result needs independent review. I decide which kind of agent is better suited to planning, implementation, or verification.

That manual interchange feels like one of the clearest hints at what comes next: a control plane above the boxes, not a better box.

A system where the computers, runtimes, browsers, and other resources underneath can increasingly be commoditized, while the layer above them figures out what the agent needs and drives the whole thing.

If tools are the hands of an agent, maybe the sandbox is its body.

But the more important question is becoming: what coordinates the body?
